Event-Driven Backtesting Engine

A backtester built the way production trading systems are built: five components that talk to each other only through a queue of events, processing one timestamp at a time. No component can see the future, because the future hasn't been pushed onto the queue yet. Roughly 300 lines of source and 72 tests.

Architecture

Five components, four messages

The natural way to backtest in pandas is vectorized: compute a signal column, shift it, multiply by returns. It's fast, and it has three structural problems — lookahead is one missing .shift(1) away, order handling doesn't map to column arithmetic, and the research code looks nothing like live code.

Why the separation matters

Strategies express views. The portfolio turns views into sized orders — this is where risk management lives. The execution handler models market frictions. In a real shop these are three systems owned by three teams, and the interfaces between them are exactly these four messages.

The engine itself is two nested loops. The outer loop is time. The inner loop is causality: one bar can cascade into a signal, an order and a fill, and all of it must settle before the bar's equity is stamped.

Lookahead is prevented structurally, not by convention

HistoricalDataHandler keeps a cursor. get_latest() slices only up to that cursor, and there is no method on the class that returns unreleased data. The strategy is not trusted to avoid the future; it is not offered the future.

That's the difference between a convention and a guarantee.

The bug worth writing a test for. Getting mark_to_market on the wrong side of the inner loop makes your equity curve lag reality by exactly one bar — which looks plausible and is wrong. An engine that marks before fills still passes a final-value check but gets bar 1 wrong, so the test suite checks bar 1 to the cent specifically.

This number is meaningless. The generator builds a drifting sine wave — cyclical by construction. A trend-following strategy on data that trends in clean regular arcs is being graded on an exam it wrote itself. Sharpe 4.10 is not evidence the strategy works; it's evidence the engine works.

Then you go to real prices and expect the number to collapse. It does: nine fills in ten years, 74% time in market, and a Sharpe of 0.75 against buy-and-hold's 0.77 — with a deeper drawdown. It took more pain for less money.

Costs are not the story

Total transaction costs across the whole run: $102 — $9 commission, $93 slippage — against $14,371 of underperformance. Anyone who blames the shortfall on frictions hasn't read the trade log.

That's an assertion until it's tested. Re-running at multiples of realistic costs answers "how wrong would the cost model have to be for this to be a cost problem":

Cost multipleSharpeReturnTotal cost

Even at 100× realistic costs — 2% one-way slippage, $0.50/share commission, nothing a real broker charges — costs reach $10,189, still short of the gap. The trade list never changes, because the strategy decides when to trade from price alone.

The whipsaws are the story. Every sell-then-buy-higher pair, priced out: 2016 −$3,956 · 2018–19 −$3,318 · 2020 COVID −$11,150 · 2022 bear +$4,542 — net −$13,882. Add $102 of costs and $486 of entry-timing drag and you have $14,470, essentially the entire $14,371 gap. The underperformance is fully explained by four decisions.

The 2020 round trip alone is 78% of it: the 50-day crossed below the 200-day on March 31, after the crash, and crossed back on July 6, after the recovery. Sold near the bottom, bought back 24% higher. That is what a lagging indicator does to a V-shaped move. The 2022 trade is the counterexample: a slow grinding bear market is exactly the regime a 50/200 crossover is built for, and there it made $4,542.

Overfitting

What happens if you do fit the windows

The grid above is careful to fit nothing, which leaves the obvious question open: you have a menu of 23 pairs and ten years of history, why not use them? Every January, score all 23 on the trailing three years, take the best by Sharpe, trade it for the next twelve months, never revise mid-year. That is a fair test — every choice is made from data that existed when the choice was made — and it loses.

Fitting the windows made it worse. Same seven years, same costs, and the tuned version finishes behind the pair nobody tuned. The training column is the tell: an in-sample optimum is the pair that best fit three years of noise, reported as though it were a property of the strategy. It also —, twice swinging between the fastest pair on the menu and the slowest — a parameter that genuinely mattered would be stable, because how long SPY trends for does not reinvent itself every January.

The hindsight card is the ceiling and it is a fiction: it is the number a grid search reports when nobody asks which data it used, and nothing in the walk-forward ever picked that pair in 2018, when picking it would have mattered. The distance between that card and the first one is the part of a tuned backtest that does not survive an unseen year — measured here rather than argued about.

Execution modeling

What filling on the close you just saw is worth

Every number above fills at the close of the bar the signal fired on. That's optimistic — you observed the close and then traded at it, which no real order can do. The conservative alternative fills at the next bar's open, so the engine implements both and runs them head to head.

Fill modelFinal equityReturnSharpe

Waiting the extra bar costs — across the ten-year run — about $125 per fill, a few basis points of overnight gap each time. Real money, and directionally exactly what you'd expect: you can never fill on a stale price to your own advantage.

Small next to the $14,371 whipsaw gap, so nothing above changes. But small isn't zero — a strategy trading far more often, or sized much larger, compounds this into something that isn't small anymore. The capacity study below puts numbers on both.

Risk management

The sizing rule nobody notices they chose

Every number above trades a fixed 200 shares. That is a decision, not the absence of one — 200 shares of SPY was $34k of exposure in 2015 and $92k in 2024, so the book's risk tripled over the sample without anyone deciding it should. Volatility targeting decides it on purpose: size the position so shares × price × trailing vol = equity × target, and re-check it while the position is open.

Identical signals in all three — same buy and sell dates, same costs. Only the share count differs.

Sizing ruleTarget volRealized volReturnSharpeMax DDFills

Realized volatility lands within a fifth of a point of the target at every setting, off nothing more than a trailing 20-day estimate. That is the mechanism validated on real data: the recent past is a good enough forecast of SPY's volatility to steer by.

Sharpe is flat across targets, and that is the correct answer. Doubling the target doubles the return and doubles the volatility, so the ratio doesn't move — exactly what leverage is supposed to do. A knob that appeared to improve Sharpe as you turned it up would be evidence of a bug, not of alpha. What it actually chooses is where on that line to sit: the 5% target gives up two thirds of the return to cut the worst drawdown from 17% to 7%.

Sharpe still went from 0.75 to 0.85, and that part is real — it comes from holding constant risk instead of accidentally-rising risk, with the trade dates untouched. It is not free either: it costs about 150 extra fills, which is 150 more chances for the liquidity assumptions below to be wrong. The rebalance_band parameter only resizes a position once it is 20% away from target, which is what keeps that number at 150 instead of 2,500.

Capacity

How much money it holds

Every number above is quoted at zero assets under management. Both fill models fill any order at any size for the same price, so a hundred shares and ten million get identical treatment. A third execution handler stops doing that: it fills at most — of a bar's volume, leaves the rest working into later bars at later prices, and charges square-root impact on every slice.

Sharpe by AUM

Max drawdown by AUM

SPY 2015–2024, fills capped at 10% of each day's volume. “No limit” is the same backtest through the unconstrained next-bar-open handler. It doesn't depend on AUM, so it is the zero-impact reference for every other point.

Impact per slice is impact_coef × trailing daily vol × sqrt(slice / bar volume). The square-root form is Almgren's; the constant in front is the part every firm calibrates on its own fills and nobody publishes, so it is a named parameter with a documented default of 1.0 instead of a number buried in the code. Anything still working when the data runs out is counted, not dropped.

One difference from the rest of the page: every row here is fully invested when long, not 200 shares. A book that keeps 60% of itself in cash has no capacity problem to study, and that is why the return and drawdown columns don't match the tables above.

Capacity is a property of turnover, not of size. Same instrument, same cap, same AUM. The difference between the two tables isn't the position, it's that one strategy trades — times in ten years and the other —. Impact is a toll paid per trade, so the AUM number on its own says very little.

The drawdown column matters more than the Sharpe column. The fast crossover's only advantage over the slow one is that it gets out of trouble sooner, and a participation cap takes that away first. It doesn't get uniformly worse at making money; it stops being the strategy it was.

Both curves recover at $100 billion, and that is noise, not a result. Nine trades, or sixty-one, isn't a sample, and a fill delay is a coin flip on each one: sometimes the price you get while working an order for months beats the one you saw. What rules those rows out isn't Sharpe, it's the slowest column. Seven months to build a position on a signal that flips every few weeks is not a strategy anyone can run, and no cost model is needed to say so.

So the ceiling for this strategy on this instrument sits between $5 and $20 billion, set by fill delay rather than by impact cost. That ceiling belongs to SPY, which is about as liquid as anything trades. The same strategy on a name doing $50 million a day would hit the same wall around —, roughly — times sooner. A capacity number quoted without the instrument's volume next to it isn't a number.

Limitations

What isn't modeled

  • Outside the capacity section, fills are complete and instant at any size. Read those numbers as an upper bound.
  • The impact constant is a default of 1.0, not a calibration. It gives the right shape of cost curve, which is enough to compare two strategies and not enough to quote a dollar figure.
  • Volume is assumed unaffected by your own trading, and the 10% cap is a constant. Real liquidity is worst exactly when you most want to trade.
  • No borrow costs, margin, or taxes.
  • Daily bars only. Intraday, the crossover dates would move.
  • Floats throughout. Real accounting systems use integer cents, because 0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3; the tests compare with tolerances instead.
  • One parameter pair (50/200) throughout. Not cherry-picked — see the grid — but also not fitted, and it shouldn't be.
  • Single asset class, one venue.
