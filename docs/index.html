One question: what price did the trade actually get?

Market Simulation Stack

A backtester and a simulated exchange, wired together. Most backtesters guess what a trade costs with a formula. This one sends the order into an order book and reads off what it got. The usual flat slippage guess is about 5x too expensive for small orders and 14x too cheap for big ones. 375 tests.

In plain English. A backtester replays history and asks "what if I had traded this way?" The weak spot is always the price you assume you got, because in real life a big order moves the price against you. I built the matching part of an exchange from scratch, then made the backtester trade through it, so the cost of each trade comes from how much is actually for sale at each price instead of from a number I picked.

Then I made the simulated book more realistic one step at a time (thin at the best price, shallower on wild days, emptier far out, and able to remember yesterday's trades). Each step changed the cost of trading. None of them changed how much money the strategy could run, which told me which number on this page was the solid one.

The result that needed both halves

Fills simulated, not assumed

The backtester used to price every trade with a formula: a flat charge, or a square-root rule with a constant that every firm tunes privately and nobody publishes. So any cost on this page was a number the reader could not check. The matching engine in exchange/ does not need a formula. It knows what the trade got, because it filled it.

src/book_execution.py builds an order book out of each day's volume, sends the order into it, and takes the average price of what the order actually consumed. Cost stops being a setting and becomes a result.

The book was not flat either, and fixing that moved the crossover 5x

Seeding every price level with the same size says the touch is as attractive a place to rest as a level ten ticks behind it. That is backwards — the touch is where you get filled by whoever knows something, so it is the least attractive place to leave size, and real books lean the other way. The shape parameter thins the front and thickens the back, holding the book's total size fixed at 30.6M shares either way.

I expected that to tilt the cost curve: small orders dearer, large orders cheaper. It does not tilt — it raises cost at every size. A fill price comes off cumulative depth, not the total, and moving size backwards lowers the cumulative at every level except the last. So the shaped book is reached into further at any size, and the two curves meet only on an order that sweeps the whole side.

So the honest description of shape is not the one I first wrote down. Normalising the total makes it size-neutral, not cost-neutral — it still moves cost, and only ever upward. That one-sidedness is the point: the knob cannot be turned to make a backtest look cheaper, only dearer, and a parameter that can only hurt the result is safer to ship than one that can flatter it. Shape 2.0 puts 11 shares at the touch, which is not a book; it is in the sweep to show the effect saturating, not as a candidate setting.

The headline number changed by a factor of five; the answer it supported changed by 0.04 of a Sharpe. Worth knowing which of your numbers are load-bearing.

Which is why decay ships off, for the opposite reason shape ships on. A shape can only make the book dearer than flat, so leaving it on is safe. A decay can make it cheaper by an amount set by a number nobody here can pin, and shipping a value from the middle of that range would quote a crossover that looked measured and was chosen. The ramp is the dearest book of the family; the one thing it cannot do is flatter the strategy. And for the third time, deployed capital did not move, because the participation cap does not know what the book looks like. The number that is robust to the cost model is the one that was never about cost.

And it was not the same book every day, which is where the money was

Both corrections above fixed where the liquidity sits. Neither touched when it is there: the book was rebuilt every bar out of that bar's volume and otherwise identical, so an average day's depth was quoted on every day in the sample — including the ones nobody wanted to quote into.

The mechanism is one sentence. A market maker quotes a budget of risk, not a number of shares. When the price is moving around more, the same number of shares is more risk, so it shows fewer of them. Depth and volatility move against each other, which is the same fact as the better-known one that spreads widen when vol rises. vol_elasticity multiplies resting size by (typical vol / current vol) ** elasticity, where current vol is trailing 20-bar realized volatility and typical vol is the expanding median of that series — what a desk sitting at that date would call a normal day, given only the history it had. Both come out of get_latest(), so a bar that has not been released cannot change a fill that has already printed; a test truncates the future and checks the price is identical.

This knob is not like shape. Shape can only make a fill dearer, so leaving it on cannot flatter a backtest. This one cuts both ways — a quiet bar gets a deeper book than the constant model gave it, and an order placed on one fills cheaper. So whether it helps or hurts is not a property of the model at all. It is a property of when the strategy trades, and that has to be measured.

Measured, it is one-sided for this strategy. Across every bar in the sample the multiplier averages 0.99 — the book has not been quietly made thinner, and a run that never traded would see no change. On the bars this strategy chose to trade it averages 0.80, and its fills land at the 64th percentile of realized volatility rather than the 50th, stable at 0.62–0.64 at every rung of the AUM ladder. The reason is not subtle once stated, which is what makes it worth stating: a moving-average crossover fires when a trend breaks, and a trend breaking is a volatility event. The signal is correlated with illiquidity by construction, and the constant-depth book cannot see any of it.

What the book remembers, and why the formula was dearer

Every table above rebuilds the book from scratch each bar: a slice that walked twenty levels on Monday finds them refilled at Monday's prices on Tuesday, and nothing the strategy does ever moves the price it trades at next. That is a model with no permanent impact at all — the one thing the square-root formula has that the book did not, and the obvious suspect for why the book's Sharpe sat above the formula's at every AUM in the table above. So the book now has a memory: persistence is the share of each slice's displacement still in the mid when the next bar's book is built, and recovery is its half-life in bars, with None meaning never, which is Almgren's permanent impact. A slice pays half of its own permanent displacement on top of what the book printed, because it accrues while the slice trades — the half in ½γX², and without it a round trip nets permanent impact to a gain.

Permanent impact is not a cost to a round trip, so it cannot be what the formula has that the book does not. With no recovery the cost per slice barely moves and the carry is negative: the strategy builds a position over weeks, pushing the mid up through every slice, then unwinds it over weeks starting from the mid it pushed up to. What the entry paid the exit gets back (Huberman & Stanzl 2004). The square-root formula charges the full displacement both ways. So the gap in the capacity table — book above formula by 0.03 to 0.08 — was never the book being generous; it is the formula charging the permanent half of impact twice.

What the formula is actually modelling is a book that heals in about a week and keeps half of what you moved. The transient rows are the ones that raise cost, because a mid that has recovered by the time the exit arrives gives nothing back. At a five-day half-life the book crosses the formula between persistence 0.33 and 0.67; at twenty days it lands on it exactly at 1.0. The formula's one unpublished constant turns out to be equivalent to a pair of book parameters in the range the propagator literature reports — a third to two thirds surviving at a day's horizon, decaying slowly. And the permanent rows carry a number not to believe: a max carried offset of 623 bps is the handler's private mid drifting six percent from the tape over a decade, which is what a never-decaying offset on top of observed prices does, since the tape already contains the permanent impact of everything that actually traded. The knob ships at 0 for the same reason decay does: both parameters are unobserved here, the memoryless book is the one setting nobody chose, and the sweep is the error bar. Deployed capital did not move on a single row — four cost corrections now, and the capacity cap is the same 41% on all of them.

The square root was in the slice, not the order

Then I tried to make the section above true at every size, not just at $5bn, and found the reason it can't be. The formula handler charges σ√(q/V) per slice. The law it cites (Almgren 2005, Tóth 2011) is σ√(Q/V) for the whole order. Those agree while the order fits in one bar. Past that every slice is the participation cap, every slice costs the same, and a position that takes a month to build costs the same per share as one that takes a day. That is a flat charge with a square root in its name — and the memoryless book that ships does exactly the same thing.

So: one order, a hundredth of a day's volume up to three days of it, worked to completion at 10% participation into the handler's book on a flat tape, so the only thing moving the price between slices is what the book remembers. So the sentence above — that the formula's constant is a pair of book parameters — held at one AUM because it had to. A curve that is flat in size and a curve that is not cross once and are not the same curve. One of the two handlers is a model of impact; the other is a constant with a square root in it, and the week-healing book matched it by being wrong by the same amount at one size. For this strategy the difference is a basis point of Sharpe at $5bn (0.51 against 0.52), because 301 slices across a decade is a position built slowly. A strategy that built one fast would be undercharged by every handler here, and that is now stated rather than tuned away.

Charge the order, and the formula lands on the book — once

Across sliced orders the exponent goes from 0.00 to 0.47 — the 2 bps of slippage that does not scale pulls it under a half; with slippage off it is 0.50 exactly. Send what you can get done — and find the book was never idle, it was stuck

The section above ended on a sizing question: a desk that knows it will get a tenth of an order done sends a tenth. So the portfolio now takes a liquidity budget — hold no more than max_fill_days × participation × ADV — and every order completes by construction. Before reading the result against the deployed column, the column had to be checked, and it was measuring the wrong thing. Deployed is one minus the shares still working when the data ran out, and on the last day of the sample a two-bar-old sell was working at every AUM above $1bn. So 2% at $100bn is how much of one order got done in two days. Averaged over every bar, with the portfolio recording its gross exposure, the $100bn book was in the market as much as the $100m one — 101% of the unconstrained rule's time in the market, against 100% at $20bn and 107% at $5bn. It was never idle. It took 161 bars to build the position and 161 to sell it, the next crossover arrived before either was done, and what rose with AUM was the Sharpe of a seven-month-lagged buy-and-hold: SPY's own over these years is 0.79, and the $100bn row's 0.75 is that with the strategy's costs out.

Make the budget bind on the way out, and run the rule that flips

Two things the section above left open. The budget capped the position at what five days could build, then sold it at whatever pace the tape allowed — which is how a five-day budget produced a sixteen-bar fill. Architecture

Five components, four messages

The natural way to backtest in pandas is vectorized: compute a signal column, shift it, multiply by returns. It's fast, and it has three structural problems — lookahead is one missing .shift(1) away, order handling doesn't map to column arithmetic, and the research code looks nothing like live code.

Why the separation matters

Strategies express views. The portfolio turns views into sized orders — this is where risk management lives. The execution handler models market frictions. In a real shop these are three systems owned by three teams, and the interfaces between them are exactly these four messages.

The engine itself is two nested loops. The outer loop is time. The inner loop is causality: one bar can cascade into a signal, an order and a fill, and all of it must settle before the bar's equity is stamped.

Lookahead is prevented structurally, not by convention

HistoricalDataHandler keeps a cursor. get_latest() slices only up to that cursor, and there is no method on the class that returns unreleased data. The strategy is not trusted to avoid the future; it is not offered the future.

That's the difference between a convention and a guarantee.

The bug worth writing a test for. Getting mark_to_market on the wrong side of the inner loop makes your equity curve lag reality by exactly one bar — which looks plausible and is wrong. An engine that marks before fills still passes a final-value check but gets bar 1 wrong, so the test suite checks bar 1 to the cent specifically.

The 2020 round trip alone is 78% of it: the 50-day crossed below the 200-day on March 31, after the crash, and crossed back on July 6, after the recovery. Sold near the bottom, bought back 24% higher. That is what a lagging indicator does to a V-shaped move. The 2022 trade is the counterexample: a slow grinding bear market is exactly the regime a 50/200 crossover is built for, and there it made $4,542.

Uncertainty

How much of this is luck?

Every Sharpe on this page is one estimate from one index over ten years, quoted to two decimals. That invites more confidence than 2,500 daily returns can support. Overfitting

What happens if you do fit the windows

The grid above is careful to fit nothing, which leaves the obvious question open: you have a menu of 23 pairs and ten years of history, why not use them? Every January, score all 23 on the trailing three years, take the best by Sharpe, trade it for the next twelve months, never revise mid-year. That is a fair test — every choice is made from data that existed when the choice was made — and it loses.

The hindsight card is the ceiling and it is a fiction: it is the number a grid search reports when nobody asks which data it used, and nothing in the walk-forward ever picked that pair in 2018, when picking it would have mattered. The distance between that card and the first one is the part of a tuned backtest that does not survive an unseen year — measured here rather than argued about.

Execution modeling

What filling on the close you just saw is worth

Every number above fills at the close of the bar the signal fired on. That's optimistic — you observed the close and then traded at it, which no real order can do. The conservative alternative fills at the next bar's open, so the engine implements both and runs them head to head.

Fill modelFinal equityReturnSharpe

Waiting the extra bar costs — across the ten-year run — about $125 per fill, a few basis points of overnight gap each time. Real money, and directionally exactly what you'd expect: you can never fill on a stale price to your own advantage.

Small next to the $14,371 whipsaw gap, so nothing above changes. But small isn't zero — a strategy trading far more often, or sized much larger, compounds this into something that isn't small anymore. The capacity study below puts numbers on both.

Risk management

The sizing rule nobody notices they chose

Every number above trades a fixed 200 shares. That is a decision, not the absence of one — 200 shares of SPY was $34k of exposure in 2015 and $92k in 2024, so the book's risk tripled over the sample without anyone deciding it should. Volatility targeting decides it on purpose: size the position so shares × price × trailing vol = equity × target, and re-check it while the position is open.

Identical signals in all three — same buy and sell dates, same costs. Only the share count differs.

Sizing ruleTarget volRealized volReturnSharpeMax DDFills

Realized volatility lands within a fifth of a point of the target at every setting, off nothing more than a trailing 20-day estimate. That is the mechanism validated on real data: the recent past is a good enough forecast of SPY's volatility to steer by.

Sharpe is flat across targets, and that is the correct answer. Doubling the target doubles the return and doubles the volatility, so the ratio doesn't move — exactly what leverage is supposed to do. A knob that appeared to improve Sharpe as you turned it up would be evidence of a bug, not of alpha. What it actually chooses is where on that line to sit: the 5% target gives up two thirds of the return to cut the worst drawdown from 17% to 7%.

Sharpe still went from 0.75 to 0.85, and that part is real — it comes from holding constant risk instead of accidentally-rising risk, with the trade dates untouched. It is not free either: it costs about 150 extra fills, which is 150 more chances for the liquidity assumptions below to be wrong. The rebalance_band parameter only resizes a position once it is 20% away from target, which is what keeps that number at 150 instead of 2,500.

Capacity

How much money it holds

Every number above is quoted at zero assets under management. Both fill models fill any order at any size for the same price, so a hundred shares and ten million get identical treatment. A third execution handler stops doing that: it fills at most — of a bar's volume, leaves the rest working into later bars at later prices, and charges square-root impact on every slice.

Sharpe by AUM

Max drawdown by AUM

SPY 2015–2024, fills capped at 10% of each day's volume. “No limit” is the same backtest through the unconstrained next-bar-open handler. It doesn't depend on AUM, so it is the zero-impact reference for every other point.

Impact per slice is impact_coef × trailing daily vol × sqrt(slice / bar volume). The square-root form is Almgren's; the constant in front is the part every firm calibrates on its own fills and nobody publishes, so it is a named parameter with a documented default of 1.0 instead of a number buried in the code. Anything still working when the data runs out is counted, not dropped.

One difference from the rest of the page: every row here is fully invested when long, not 200 shares. A book that keeps 60% of itself in cash has no capacity problem to study, and that is why the return and drawdown columns don't match the tables above.

Capacity is a property of turnover, not of size. Same instrument, same cap, same AUM. The difference between the two tables isn't the position, it's that one strategy trades — times in ten years and the other —. Impact is a toll paid per trade, so the AUM number on its own says very little.

The drawdown column matters more than the Sharpe column. The fast crossover's only advantage over the slow one is that it gets out of trouble sooner, and a participation cap takes that away first. It doesn't get uniformly worse at making money; it stops being the strategy it was.

Both curves recover at $100 billion, and that is noise, not a result. Nine trades, or sixty-one, isn't a sample, and a fill delay is a coin flip on each one: sometimes the price you get while working an order for months beats the one you saw. What rules those rows out isn't Sharpe, it's the slowest column. Seven months to build a position on a signal that flips every few weeks is not a strategy anyone can run, and no cost model is needed to say so.

So the ceiling for this strategy on this instrument sits between $5 and $20 billion, set by fill delay rather than by impact cost. That ceiling belongs to SPY, which is about as liquid as anything trades. The same strategy on a name doing $50 million a day would hit the same wall around —, roughly — times sooner. A capacity number quoted without the instrument's volume next to it isn't a number.

Limitations

What isn't modeled

  • Outside the capacity section, fills are complete and instant at any size. Read those numbers as an upper bound.
  • The impact constant is a default of 1.0, not a calibration. It gives the right shape of cost curve, which is enough to compare two strategies and not enough to quote a dollar figure.
  • Volume is assumed unaffected by your own trading, and the 10% cap is a constant. Depth is no longer a constant — it thins with volatility, which is where that last sentence turned out to be worth 19% of this strategy's trading cost — but volume should rise as depth falls, and only one half of that is modelled.
  • No borrow costs, margin, or taxes.
  • One asset. cross_section.py runs the same rule across 24 ETFs — see above. Every other number on this page is still SPY alone.
  • Daily bars only. Intraday, the crossover dates would move.
  • Floats throughout. Real accounting systems use integer cents, because 0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3; the tests compare with tolerances instead.
  • One parameter pair (50/200) throughout. Not cherry-picked — see the grid — but also not fitted, and it shouldn't be.
  • Single asset class, one venue.
